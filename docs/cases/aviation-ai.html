Experience-based case study · Aviation

Etihad Airways — AI-enabled fuel-efficiency transformation.

I advised Etihad Airways leadership on translating its fuel-efficiency objectives into an AI programme with a prioritised use-case portfolio, a business case and an implementation roadmap. My contribution connected the investment decision to the technical requirements and operating changes needed to turn model outputs into useful recommendations.

The platform combines fuel prediction, anomaly detection and flight optimisation, with agentic orchestration and reinforcement learning within the engineering scope. The business objective is to identify credible opportunities to reduce fuel consumption, assess feasible interventions and help operational teams decide where to act.

The business problem

A flight that burns more than expected is a reason to investigate, not a saving.

Fuel consumption reflects a mixture of operating conditions and decisions. Route length, payload, weather, aircraft condition, air-traffic restrictions and ground operations can all affect performance. The difference between actual and expected burn cannot automatically be treated as recoverable fuel.

The investment question was therefore specific: could the airline identify a material opportunity, connect it to an action within an operator's control, deliver the recommendation at the right time and measure the incremental benefit? That question shaped the use cases, the solution design and the delivery plan.

My remit

Opportunity identification and prioritisation, the business and value case, data and technology requirements, decision-support design, governance, the operating model and implementation support — connecting specialist work to executive choices about scope, investment, readiness and ownership.

The AI and data-science team's remit

Detailed model development and the engineering of agents, training, evaluation, observability and deployment. My role was to make the business requirements precise enough to build, challenge whether the proposed solution could support the operating decision, and coordinate the dependencies implementation required.

The product the programme is trying to produce

An operational user receives a prioritised opportunity with supporting flight context, likely drivers, a feasible action, estimated fuel impact, uncertainty, and the conditions under which the recommendation applies.

Leadership receives a view of adoption, verified benefits and the decisions needed to extend or revise the programme.

Results

The engagement created the structure required to move from AI ambition into implementation.

The strongest measurable outcomes I can show publicly are programme and implementation outcomes rather than confidential fuel-savings figures.

Defining the investment and the operating decision

Each use case was framed around the decision it would improve.

That made it possible to compare value, data readiness, technical feasibility and adoption requirements on the same basis. It also exposed dependencies: reliable prediction and a usable review process underpin many of the optimisation opportunities.

Prioritisation and the value case

I assessed opportunities against business value, AI advantage, data readiness, actionability, feasibility, adoption and scalability. A high potential saving required scrutiny of the practical route to realisation: who could act, whether the data arrived in time, how often the situation occurred, and what operating change was needed.

This kept foundational capabilities and direct benefit claims distinct. A better prediction can enable a valuable decision; its accuracy improvement alone is not a fuel-saving outcome. Similarly, reinforcement learning belongs where sequential decisions justify the additional modelling, evaluation and support effort — not wherever it would be interesting to apply.

A design review that changes the build

When actual fuel burn differs from an expected baseline, the first question is whether the comparison is valid: same fuel definition, comparable route and operating conditions, and no post-decision information leaking into the analysis.

The next question is whether a feasible intervention exists. A predictive signal is useful only when the programme can connect it to a controllable lever, a decision window and an accountable user.

The output of that review is a defined user, decision point, evidence requirement and escalation path. That is the point at which a broad analytics use case becomes a product the delivery team can implement.

Translating use cases into a production architecture

Specialist numerical models, agent orchestration and operational authority are separate responsibilities.

I translated the priority use cases into requirements across data, analytics, technology and workflow. The design keeps those responsibilities apart so each component has an explicit purpose and can be evaluated independently.

Data that is valid at the point of use

The specification needs a flight identifier, aligned time references, consistent fuel units and a defined target. For each feature it records the source, owner, arrival time, transformation, missing-data tolerance and lineage.

A field available after landing may support diagnosis but cannot enter a pre-flight model as though it were already known.

Models with defined inputs and outputs

Prediction services return estimates and uncertainty. Optimisation services return feasible candidate actions, their objective values and constraint results.

An agent can select tools and summarise evidence; fuel calculations and operational constraints require validated numerical services and explicit checks outside the language model.

Model flexibility with controlled change

A model-agnostic design needs stable interfaces, versioned inputs and outputs, and regression evaluations. Replacing a predictor, optimiser or language model must preserve the behaviour downstream users expect.

Development and deployment remain separate steps, with approval, rollback and an auditable record of the version behind each recommendation.

Observability tied to the decision

A useful trace links the data snapshot, the model and agent versions, tool responses, constraint checks, the recommendation, the reviewer's action and the later outcome.

That is what lets the team distinguish a data failure from a model error, an agent failure, or a recommendation that simply arrived too late to use.

Technical judgment behind the recommendations

The consequential technical arguments are about whether evidence supports an operating action.

These are the review questions that connect the platform design to the airline's decision and its value case. They are also, in my experience, where a programme is either made rigorous or quietly allowed to drift.

01

A strong average score can hide an unusable model

Evaluation should compare the model with a meaningful existing baseline on later, unseen data. I look for error in fuel units, systematic bias, and performance by route, fleet, airport and operating regime. Where generalisation to new routes or aircraft is claimed, the evaluation must test that claim explicitly.

Uncertainty also needs a test. An interval labelled as 80 percent should be assessed for observed coverage, including on material segments. Temporal shift can undermine calibration. When inputs fall outside the validated population, or uncertainty is too wide, the workflow needs a review or abstention path.
02

An explanation is not proof that an action will save fuel

Feature importance and Shapley attribution explain a fitted model. They do not establish that changing a variable will cause the predicted saving. A counterfactual must preserve operating context, change only feasible levers, and avoid combinations unsupported by the data.

My decision-support framing therefore separates a likely driver, a proposed intervention, and evidence of incremental impact. Operational review tests feasibility; a designed pilot tests the intervention. Taxi delays caused by external congestion, for example, cannot simply be converted into an action owned by the crew.
03

Optimisation and reinforcement learning need distinct tests

A constraint-based optimiser needs an agreed objective, permitted decision variables and hard operating limits. Its recommendations should be compared with existing policies and tested for sensitivity to uncertain inputs. Improving the objective while violating a hard constraint is a failed recommendation, not a marginal one.

For reinforcement learning the additional questions concern the state, action space, reward, time horizon and evaluation environment. A policy that scores well in simulation can exploit simulator weaknesses or fail under a different operating distribution. Offline and simulation evidence, domain validation and comparison with simpler approaches must precede any operational use.
04

Agent quality must be assessed across the full task

A fluent explanation can conceal an incorrect tool call or an unsupported claim. Agent evaluation should test tool selection, parameter correctness, grounding in returned evidence, handling of missing data, and completion within the decision window. Repeated calls, tool failures and stale results also need bounded behaviour.

The acceptance condition is a recommendation with valid evidence and permitted actions, or a clear escalation. That gives engineering a testable requirement and gives operations an understandable reason to accept, challenge or decline the output.

Managing the path to production

The central dependency is an end-to-end operating decision.

Data must arrive, the model must perform, the recommendation must be feasible, and the intended user must be able to act. I connected business, data, AI, technology, governance and adoption into one implementation roadmap around that dependency.

The delivery model builds a complete slice for a bounded use case before extending scope. The same slice carries data validation, model evaluation, integration, user review, outcome capture and support requirements — which exposes integration and adoption problems while they can still change the design.

Working level and executive decisions

Working-team delivery tracking connects each output to its owner, dependency, acceptance evidence and next decision. Technical reviews examine model behaviour, data quality, failure cases and change risk. Executive steering addresses only the choices that change value, scope, cost, timing or long-term ownership.

An escalation, done properly

If a critical data feed arrives after the required decision time, the issue is larger than a delayed integration task. The choices are to improve the feed, narrow the feature set, move the decision window, or change the use case. The escalation should quantify the implications of each and recommend a path.

Ownership after deployment

The operating model needs clear responsibility for source data, models, orchestration, infrastructure, user support and benefits measurement.

A monitoring alert must lead to an action: investigate the feed, restrict recommendations, revert a release, or assess retraining. Retraining should pass evaluation and release approval before it changes production behaviour.

Measuring value

Benefits that can support an investment decision.

I developed the value case around the share of opportunity the airline could identify, act on and realise. The assumptions need to remain visible: eligible operations, fuel opportunity, actionable share, adoption, persistence of effect, fuel price, and implementation and operating costs.

Realised benefits require an agreed comparison with what would otherwise have happened. Weather, payload, route mix, aircraft changes and regression to the mean can all distort a simple before-and-after comparison. The pilot should capture accepted and rejected recommendations, the reasons, and subsequent outcomes, with Finance validating the attribution method. Overlapping use cases must not count the same saving twice.
